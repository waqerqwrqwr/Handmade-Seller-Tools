5 Pricing Mistakes Small Handmade Sellers Make

Five common problems to check before publishing a handmade listing.

Pricing errors often look harmless on one product. Repeated across dozens of orders, they can quietly reduce the money your business keeps. Here are five mistakes worth checking.

1. Pricing from materials alone

Materials are visible, so they are easy to count. Labor and selling costs are less obvious, but they still affect the economics of the product.

2. Treating your labor as free

If a product takes an hour to make, that hour has an opportunity cost. Even a simple internal hourly rate can make your pricing decisions more realistic.

3. Forgetting selling fees

Marketplace and payment fees can take a meaningful share of a sale. They should be part of the pricing calculation, not an afterthought.

4. Using competitor prices as your formula

Competitor prices can provide market context, but they do not tell you your own costs. Two sellers can offer similar products while having very different labor, sourcing, and overhead structures.

5. Looking at revenue instead of profit

A strong-looking sales number does not automatically mean a strong business. Track the money left after relevant costs and compare products on a consistent basis.

A simple pre-listing check

  • Do I know my complete per-unit cost?
  • Did I include my labor?
  • Did I account for selling fees?
  • Is the target profit deliberate?
  • Can I repeat the calculation for the next product?

Mistake 6: rounding down to a tidy number

Round numbers are easy to say and easy to underprice. Suppose a careful calculation produces a price of $27.40. Rounding down to $25.00 feels harmless, but the $2.40 you give up per sale is real profit, not a discount you can absorb for free.

Across 180 sales a year, $2.40 is $432 of profit gone, traded away in a decision that took two seconds. Rounding up has the opposite effect: $27.40 becomes $28.00, which adds $0.60 per sale, or $108 across the same 180 sales. Neither direction is automatically right, but make the choice deliberately and know what it costs. If $2.40 a unit matters to your total, do not hand it over by reflex.

Mistake 7: setting a price and never revisiting it

Costs drift. Suppose your main material rises 12%, from $5.00 to $5.60 per unit, which is $0.60 more per item. On 15 units a week that is $9.00 a week, or about $468 a year. A price set two years ago and never rechecked simply absorbs that cost until it eats the margin.

The same works in the other direction. If you change suppliers and save $0.40 per unit, that saving only reaches your pocket if you know your current cost. A price you can trust is one you have checked recently, against today’s materials and today’s fees.

When a lower price is a deliberate choice

Not every low price is a mistake. A deliberately low price can make sense for a sample, a first-time-buyer offer, a bundle, or a product that draws customers to your stall where they buy other things. The difference between a strategy and a mistake is whether you know the trade-off.

If you price a $6.00 bar of soap at $4.00 as an introductory offer, you give up $2.00 a bar. Selling 50 of them gives up $100 of potential profit. That is fine if those buyers return, and a loss if they do not, but only if you counted the $100 and chose to spend it. A low price you chose with your eyes open is a marketing decision; a low price you drifted into is a pricing mistake.

Keep it bounded: set a time limit or a quantity for the offer, then return to your normal price.

A short routine that keeps pricing honest

Most pricing mistakes come from skipping a check, not from a bad formula. A short routine catches them:

  • After a cost change, re-run the price for every product that uses that material.
  • Once a quarter, rebuild one or two prices from scratch and compare them to what you currently charge.
  • Before publishing a listing, confirm the price covers materials, labor, and selling fees — not materials alone.
  • Keep the same cost definitions across products so comparisons mean something.

None of this takes long. The point is to make the check a habit rather than something you reach for only after the numbers already look wrong.

One habit matters more than any single calculation: write down why a price is what it is. If you know the material cost, the labor time, and the fee assumption behind a number, you can explain it to a customer and adjust it the moment one of those inputs moves. A price with no reasoning recorded behind it is the easiest mistake of all to repeat without noticing.
