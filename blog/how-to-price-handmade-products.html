How to Price Handmade Products Without Losing Money

A practical framework for independent handmade sellers.

Pricing a handmade product starts with a complete view of what it actually costs you to make and sell it. A materials-only calculation can make a product look profitable when your time, packaging, transaction costs, or other expenses are included.

1. Start with direct product costs

Add the costs that clearly belong to one product: materials, components, packaging, and product-specific production expenses. The goal is to get a realistic cost for one finished unit.

2. Put a value on your labor

Estimate how long the product takes to make and multiply that time by the hourly rate you want the business to recognize. Your labor does not become free simply because you work for yourself.

3. Add the costs of selling

Include marketplace fees, payment processing, advertising, shipping subsidies, and other per-sale costs that reduce what you keep. These costs are easy to ignore when you look only at the listed price.

4. Decide how much profit you want

Once the full cost is clear, choose a target margin or profit amount. The exact target depends on your product, market, and business model, but the important thing is that it is deliberate rather than accidental.

5. Make the method repeatable

A reusable calculator keeps your pricing logic consistent. When a material price changes or a marketplace fee changes, you can update the inputs instead of rebuilding the calculation every time.

A worked example: pricing a soy candle

Say a soy candle uses $4.20 of wax, fragrance, and wick, plus $1.10 for the jar. That is $5.30 of direct materials. Packaging — a lid, a label, and a shipping box — adds $0.85, so the product itself costs $5.30 + $0.85 = $6.15 before any labor.

Suppose you can pour four candles in an hour, so each candle carries 15 minutes of production time. At an internal labor rate of $18 per hour, that is $4.50 per candle. Adding it to $6.15 gives a pre-selling cost of $10.65.

Now the cost of selling. Assume your marketplace and payment fees together run about 10% of the sale price, and you set advertising at $1.50 per unit. On a $28 list price those come to $2.80 + $1.50 = $4.30. Total cost is $10.65 + $4.30 = $14.95, leaving a profit of $28.00 - $14.95 = $13.05, a margin of about 46.6%. Because fees scale with the price, treat this as a first pass and re-run it once you settle on a number.

Markup vs. margin: two ways to set a price

Markup and margin both describe the gap between cost and price, but they are measured against different bases, and mixing them up leads to underpricing. Markup is profit divided by cost. Margin is profit divided by price.

For the candle, a cost of $10.65 and a price of $28.00 give a profit of $17.35 before selling costs, which is a markup of about 163% and a margin of about 62%. Once selling costs are included, the margin falls to 46.6%.

The distinction matters because a 50% markup and a 50% margin are very different numbers. A 50% markup on $10.65 is $10.65 x 1.5 = $15.98. After roughly 10% in fees and $1.50 of advertising, that leaves about $2.23 of profit — a 14% margin. A true 50% margin would need a price of $10.65 / 0.5 = $21.30 before selling costs, and more once fees are added. Decide which one you mean and write it down, because 50% on its own is ambiguous.

Pricing for wholesale and craft fairs

Wholesale asks you to accept a smaller slice of the retail price in exchange for volume and a single buyer. A common convention is to sell to a shop at roughly half of retail, but the exact figure depends on the buyer and your own costs, so test it against your numbers rather than assuming.

If the candle retails at $28.00 and a shop offers $14.00, that leaves $14.00 - $10.65 = $3.35 per unit before selling costs. Wholesale orders often avoid marketplace fees and advertising, which helps, but $3.35 per candle means you need real volume for the order to be worth your time. Never accept a wholesale price below your pre-selling cost — below that line, every unit you ship loses money.

Craft fairs sit in between. You keep the full retail price but pay a booth fee, travel, and a day of your time. If a fair costs $80 and you sell 30 candles, that is $80 / 30 = $2.67 per candle in booth cost, before you count the hours you stand there.

Reviewing your prices when costs change

Wax, fragrance, and shipping rates move. A price that worked in spring can quietly become unprofitable by autumn. Keep a short record of the inputs behind your price — materials, packaging, labor rate, and selling costs — and review it on a fixed schedule, for example every three months.

Here is the test. If your wax supplier raises prices by 15%, the wax portion of the candle rises from $4.20 to $4.83, an increase of $0.63 per candle. Across 200 candles a month that is $0.63 x 200 = $126 of extra cost. A price increase of $0.65 per candle covers it exactly at your current volume; you can then decide whether to absorb the cost, raise the price, or find another supplier.

Because a calculator keeps every input in one place, that decision takes a few minutes instead of an afternoon of re-adding the numbers on paper.
