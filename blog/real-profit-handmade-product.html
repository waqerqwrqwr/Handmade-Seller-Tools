PROFIT GUIDE

How to Calculate the Real Profit on a Handmade Product

Why sales revenue and real profit are not the same number.

A product can sell for $40 and still produce much less than $40 of usable profit. Revenue is the money collected from a sale; profit is what remains after the costs required to make and sell that sale are accounted for.

Start with revenue

For a simple per-unit calculation, begin with the selling price actually paid by the customer. If you offer discounts, use the price after the discount rather than the original list price.

Build the full cost

Add the variable costs connected to the order. Typical examples include materials, packaging, direct labor, transaction fees, marketplace fees, and product-specific shipping costs.

Calculate what remains

A basic formula is:

Profit = Revenue − Total Costs
Use the same cost definitions consistently so you can compare products fairly.

Turn profit into a margin

Profit margin can be expressed as profit divided by revenue. This makes it easier to compare products with different price points.

Why this matters for product selection

Once you calculate profit consistently, you can see which products deserve more attention, which prices need adjustment, and which products consume too much time relative to what they return.

A worked example: two products compared

Numbers get abstract quickly, so compare two products side by side. Both are sold at the same craft fair.

Product A, a zip pouch, sells for $24.00. Materials are $6.00, packaging $0.60, and it takes 20 minutes to sew — $6.00 of labor at $18 per hour. Payment and shipping costs add $1.20. Total cost is $6.00 + $0.60 + $6.00 + $1.20 = $13.80, leaving a profit of $24.00 - $13.80 = $10.20 and a margin of 42.5%.

Product B, a tote bag, sells for $38.00. Materials are $9.50, packaging $0.60, and it takes 45 minutes, which is $13.50 of labor. Selling costs run to $1.90. Total cost is $9.50 + $0.60 + $13.50 + $1.90 = $25.50, leaving $38.00 - $25.50 = $12.50 of profit and a margin of about 32.9%.

On profit per unit the tote wins, by $12.50 - $10.20 = $2.30. Whether that makes it the better product depends on the next number.

Profit per hour is the comparison that decides

A product earns profit per unit, but your business only has so many hours in it. Divide each product’s profit by the time it takes to make.

The pouch produces $10.20 in 20 minutes, which is $10.20 x 3 = $30.60 per hour. The tote produces $12.50 in 45 minutes, which is $12.50 / 0.75 = $16.67 per hour. Measured that way, the pouch earns almost twice as much for every hour you put in.

The tote is not a bad product — it earns more per sale — but it uses your time less efficiently. This is why the question of which product is more profitable has two honest answers: per unit, the tote wins; per hour of your work, the pouch wins. Which one matters more depends on how much time you have and whether one product brings customers who then buy the other.

Break-even: how many units must you sell

Per-unit profit becomes a business answer when you set it against fixed costs — the expenses you pay whether or not you sell anything, such as a website, software, or a craft-fair table you have already booked.

Suppose fixed costs are $300 a month. With the pouch’s $10.20 of profit, you cover it at $300 / $10.20 = 29.4, so 30 pouches. With the tote’s $12.50, it is $300 / $12.50 = 24 totes. Break-even already counts your labor inside the cost, so this is the point where the business covers its bills, not the point where you are paid anything extra.

If you sell a mix, add the profit contributions together. Ten pouches contribute $102.00 and fifteen totes contribute $187.50, for $289.50 — just short of the $300 line, which tells you precisely how many more sales you need.

Check profit across a month, not one sale

A single sale tells you whether a product is viable. A month tells you whether the business is. At the end of each month, total your revenue and subtract the costs you actually recorded — materials, fees, shipping, and fixed costs.

Small surprises add up: a jump in shipping costs, a sale that discounted prices, or a few returns can pull the month’s real margin below what any single order suggested. That gap is the reason a per-unit figure is a starting point rather than the final word.

Keep the monthly check simple enough that you will actually do it. A short total of revenue, variable costs, and fixed costs is enough to see the trend, and it gives you facts to base a price change on instead of a guess.
