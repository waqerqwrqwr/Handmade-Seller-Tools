A craft fair inverts the usual pricing problem. Online, each sale carries a fee but few fixed costs; at an event, the booth is paid whether you sell one item or a hundred. This calculator works out the contribution each sale makes, spreads the event's fixed costs across the units you actually expect to sell, and shows how many items you need to break even.

That break-even unit count is the number worth remembering. It turns an event into a target you can plan stock against, rather than a hope that the day goes well.

How to price for a craft fair

Event pricing has three moving parts that online pricing does not: a large fixed cost, a payment fee on every card sale, and a stack of inventory that is expensive to carry home.

  • Own the fixed cost first. Add the booth fee, travel, parking, table and display rental, and any helpers. That total is what the event has to earn back before you see a cent.
  • Know your contribution per unit. Selling price minus direct unit cost minus the payment fee is what each sale adds toward covering the fixed cost.
  • Plan a realistic sales volume. Selling every item is rare. Estimate on the cautious side and check whether the booth still covers its costs.
  • Leave room to negotiate. In-person buyers ask for deals, bundle purchases, and expect a little flexibility. The sticker price should carry that room without dropping below your contribution target.

What a craft fair price has to cover

A fair price carries costs an online listing never sees:

  • Booth or event fees — sometimes plus a percentage of sales, or a membership charge.
  • Travel and setup — fuel, accommodation, meals, and the time to load, set up, and pack down.
  • Direct unit cost — the same make-or-buy cost as any other channel.
  • Card and payment processing — a percentage of each sale, and often a small fixed amount per transaction.
  • Cash-handling costs — the change float, and the occasional mistake or loss that in-person selling brings.
  • Unsold inventory — stock you carry home is money the event did not recover.

How this calculator works

Event profit = (selling price − direct unit cost − payment fee) × units sold − fixed event costs

The term in brackets is the contribution each sale makes. Multiply it by the units you sell, then subtract the fixed event cost. The metrics panel also shows the break-even unit count — the fixed cost divided by the contribution, rounded up — which is the point at which the event stops losing money.

Worked examples

Using the defaults — $360 in fixed event costs, $9 direct cost, $30 selling price, and a 3% payment fee:

  • Selling the full 60 units → contribution $20.10 each, profit about $846.00, and a break-even of 18 units.
  • A slow day at 25 units → profit still positive at about $142.50, because the contribution is comfortably above the fixed cost headroom.
  • A crowded category: $500 fixed costs, $12 cost, $18 price, 40 units → contribution $5.46, a loss of about $281.60, and a break-even of 92 units.
  • A bigger event: $800 fixed costs, $9 cost, $32 price, 90 units → contribution $22.04, profit about $1,183.60, break-even 37 units.

The third example is the cautionary one. A small contribution against a large booth fee can mean the event is unprofitable even on a busy day; the fix is a higher price, a lower unit cost, or a cheaper booth.

Common mistakes when pricing for a fair

  • Forgetting the booth fee. Pricing each item as if it sold online ignores the fixed cost that the event must pay back first.
  • Leaving out card fees. A 3% fee on every card sale is small per transaction but adds up across a busy weekend, and refusing cards forfeits sales.
  • Pricing with no room to negotiate. If your sticker price is already your floor, you cannot offer the bundle or the small discount that closes a sale without losing money.
  • Over-stocking. Bringing three times the break-even quantity ties up cash in inventory that may come home unsold, and unsold stock is not free.
  • Assuming a strong turnout. Plan the break-even against a cautious sales estimate, not the optimistic one, so a quiet day does not turn into a loss.

How to use the result

  • Take the break-even unit count into the event as your target, and track sales against it through the day.
  • Test a couple of price points — the calculator shows how a small change in price moves both the profit and the break-even count.
  • Compare the fair's profit with the same inventory sold online, and decide where your production time earns more.

Important assumptions

The estimate depends entirely on your inputs. Fixed costs, unit costs, prices, and the payment fee are assumptions, and the units-sold figure is a forecast rather than a result. The model does not include sales tax, booth-fee percentages, unsold-inventory write-downs, or your own time at the event. Treat the break-even count as a planning target and check it against your real numbers once the fair is over.

How do I know if a craft fair is worth it?

Estimate the contribution each sale makes, subtract the event's fixed costs, and test a realistic sales volume. If the break-even unit count is far below what you can reasonably sell, the event is likely worthwhile.

Should craft fair prices differ from my online prices?

They often should. In-person selling may avoid shipping but adds booth and card fees, and buyers expect the freedom to negotiate. Compare the total cost of each channel before settling on a price.

How many units should I bring to a fair?

A workable starting point is the break-even count plus a margin for the best-case day, but not so much that unsold stock ties up cash. Bring more of your proven sellers and fewer of the untested items.

Does the calculator include card processing fees?

Yes, as a percentage you enter. If your processor also charges a small fixed amount per transaction, add it to the direct unit cost so the contribution stays accurate.

What counts as a fixed event cost?

Everything you pay regardless of how much you sell: the booth fee, travel, parking, display rental, and any helper costs. Sum them and enter the total.

Why is my break-even higher than my planned stock?

A high break-even usually means the contribution per unit is low or the fixed cost is high. Raising the price, reducing the unit cost, or booking a cheaper booth all lower the number of sales you need.
