Handmade pricing is harder than it looks, because the biggest cost is often invisible: your own time. This calculator builds a price from the full cost of one item — materials, labor, packaging, shipping, and a share of overhead — and then applies either a markup or a target margin so you can see both ways of reaching a number.

It is meant for makers who want a price they can defend, not a price that merely feels fair. Enter honest inputs and the result shows what the item actually has to sell for in order to pay for itself and leave something behind.

How to price a handmade product

The reliable path runs from cost, to labor, to a pricing rule, then to a market check. Working in that order stops you from anchoring on a competitor's price before you know your own floor.

  • Cost the materials per item. Not the price of the spool or the sheet — the amount actually used in one finished piece, including thread, glue, and hardware.
  • Value your time. Record the hours of making, finishing, photographing, and packing, then multiply by an hourly rate you would accept for that work.
  • Add packaging and shipping. The box, the tissue, the label, and the shipping you absorb are all part of the cost of a sale.
  • Allocate overhead. Studio rent or utilities, tool wear, subscription software, and the consumables that disappear over time belong in each item as a small per-unit figure.

What to include in a handmade item's cost

The cost of a handmade item is more than the material bill. A practical checklist:

  • Materials, measured per item — including the waste you cannot avoid.
  • Consumables — glue, thread, sandpaper, glaze, ink, and any supply that runs out without becoming part of the piece.
  • Labor — hours multiplied by your hourly rate.
  • Packaging — the outer box, the inner wrap, and any insert.
  • Seller-paid shipping — the real postage and any handling time it costs you.
  • Overhead — a fair share of the fixed costs of running the workshop.

How this calculator works

Total cost = materials + labor + packaging + shipping + overhead; markup price = total cost × (1 + markup); margin price = total cost ÷ (1 − margin)

Two pricing rules, two different answers. Markup adds a percentage on top of cost; margin divides, because margin is profit as a share of the selling price. The main result uses your markup, and the metrics panel shows the margin-based price beside it so you can compare.

Worked examples

Using the defaults — $7 materials, 0.75 hours at $20/hour, $1.50 packaging, $3 shipping, and $1 overhead:

  • Total cost is $27.50. At a 100% markup the price is $55.00; the same item at a 35% margin prices at about $42.31.
  • A small item: $3.00 materials, 0.5 hours at $22/hour, $0.80 packaging, $4.00 shipping, $0.50 overhead → cost $19.30; 150% markup → $48.25; 40% margin → about $32.17.
  • A larger piece: $18.00 materials, 2 hours at $25/hour, $2.50 packaging, no shipping, $3.00 overhead → cost $73.50; 80% markup → $132.30.
  • A low-cost item: $1.20 materials, 0.2 hours at $18/hour, $0.40 packaging, $2.00 shipping, $0.30 overhead → cost $7.50; 200% markup → $22.50; 50% margin → $15.00.

Notice how the same cost can justify very different prices depending on the rule. Setting both does the most important job in handmade pricing: it forces you to see your time as a line item rather than a hidden subsidy.

Common mistakes when pricing handmade goods

  • Paying yourself nothing for labor. Leaving time out of the cost is the single most common error, and it hides a price that quietly loses money on every sale.
  • Ignoring consumables. Glue, thread, finish, and failed attempts are consumed even when they never appear in the finished piece, so they belong in the cost.
  • Using the old "materials times two" rule. Doubling materials can work for mass-produced goods, but it ignores labor and overhead — the two costs that dominate handmade work.
  • Confusing markup with margin. A 100% markup is a 50% margin. Pricing to one while expecting the other leads to prices that are either too high or too low.
  • Forgetting overhead. Tools, studio costs, and subscriptions are real expenses that have to be recovered across the items you sell.

How to use the result

  • Compare the markup price and the margin price, then pick the rule that fits your market and hold to it consistently across a product line.
  • Raise your hourly rate or your overhead allocation and watch the price move — if a small change makes the item unsellable, the problem is usually cost, not pricing.
  • Sense-check the number against comparable handmade listings. If your price sits far above them, look for material or time savings before you cut your rate.

Important assumptions

The result is a planning estimate built entirely from the inputs you enter. Labor is priced at your chosen hourly rate, not a market wage, and overhead is an allocation you set rather than a measured figure. The model does not include marketplace or payment fees, sales tax, discounts, or returns — subtract those from the margin if you sell through a channel that charges them. Revisit the numbers whenever material costs, shipping rates, or your own hourly expectation change.

Should I include my labor when pricing handmade items?

Yes. Your production time is a real business cost, and skipping it means the sale pays you nothing for the hours you spent. Enter the hours and the hourly rate you want the business to pay you.

What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is profit as a percentage of cost; margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price. A 100% markup equals a 50% margin, so the same item can look very different under each rule.

How do I set an hourly rate for my own work?

Start from the rate you would need to pay someone else to do the same work, then adjust for your skill and local expectations. The number you choose is an assumption you control, not an official figure.

How do I estimate overhead per item?

Add up your fixed costs for a period — studio rent, utilities, tools, software, insurance — and divide by the number of items you expect to make in that period. The result is your per-item allocation.

Why are the markup price and the margin price different?

Because the two rules measure profit from opposite directions. Markup multiplies the cost, while margin divides it by (1 − margin), which always produces a higher price for the same percentage.

Does this include Etsy or payment fees?

No. The model prices the item itself. If you sell through a marketplace or take card payments, subtract those fees from your margin or add them to the cost before setting the final price.
