Markup is the amount you add on top of what a product costs, expressed as a percentage of that cost. It is the pricing tool most makers use day to day, because it starts from a number you already know — your unit cost — and produces a selling price. This calculator turns a cost and a markup percentage into a selling price, the markup amount, and the implied profit margin.

What markup measures

Markup answers "how much did I add?" rather than "what share did I keep?" A 75% markup on a $12 item adds $9 and yields a $21 price. That is a clear, repeatable rule: whatever an item costs, multiply by 1.75. The result is a price, and the markup amount is the gross profit before any other cost.

Because markup is anchored to cost, it scales with your material and labour inputs. When a supplier raises prices by 10%, a fixed markup rule keeps your dollar profit proportional — but it also means your percentage margin drifts, which is why markup and margin need to be watched together.

Markup vs. margin — the conversion

Markup and margin share a numerator (profit) but divide by different bases (cost versus selling price). That single difference explains every "why don't these match?" question in pricing:

  • A 100% markup doubles the price and produces a 50% margin.
  • A 50% markup produces about a 33.3% margin.
  • A 25% markup produces a 20% margin.
  • Margins approach 100% only as markup grows without limit — the two never meet.

The conversion runs both ways: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup), and markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin). If you know the margin a product must hit to cover overhead, you can solve for the markup that delivers it instead of guessing.

The formulas

Selling price = cost × (1 + markup); markup amount = cost × markup; implied margin = markup amount ÷ selling price × 100

Two details are easy to miss. The implied margin shown here is calculated on the selling price alone — it does not yet subtract marketplace fees, shipping, or packaging. And a markup of zero leaves no profit at all, so the implied margin is zero as well.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing markup with margin. A 50% markup is a 33.3% margin, always. Treating them as interchangeable overstates profit.
  • Assuming a 100% markup means double the profit. It doubles the price, not the profit — half of the new price is still the original cost.
  • Applying markup to the selling price. Markup is always applied to cost; applying it to the price quietly shrinks the price you intended.
  • Stopping at gross profit. The markup amount ignores seller fees, packing, and shipping, so it is a ceiling on profit rather than the final figure.
  • Leaving markup fixed while costs rise. A flat percentage keeps the ratio constant but does not guarantee the dollar margin stays healthy once fees scale with price.

How to use the result

  • Translate a markup rule into a concrete price before listing, so the number is deliberate rather than estimated.
  • Check the implied margin against the margin your business needs, and raise the markup if it falls short.
  • Keep a short table of your common cost points and their markup prices for fast quoting at markets and fairs.

Important assumptions

Results are planning estimates. The selling price reflects cost plus markup and nothing else, so platform fees, payment processing, shipping, and taxes are not deducted. The implied margin is a theoretical ceiling that only becomes real profit after every other cost is paid. Replace the defaults with your actual unit costs, and revisit the numbers when material prices, fees, or your target margin change.

How do I calculate a markup percentage?

Markup is profit divided by cost, multiplied by 100. If an item costs $12 and sells for $21, the profit is $9 and the markup is 9 ÷ 12 = 75%. To price from a markup, multiply cost by one plus the rate.

What does a 100% markup mean?

It means the markup amount equals the cost, so the selling price is double the cost. The margin on that price is 50%, not 100% — half the price is still the original cost.

How do I convert markup to margin?

Divide the markup by one plus the markup: a 100% markup becomes 1.0 ÷ 2.0 = 50%. To go the other way, divide the margin by one minus the margin. A 40% margin converts to a markup of about 66.7%.

What markup should I use?

There is no universal figure. It depends on your costs, the fees your sales channel charges, and the margin your business needs. Start from the margin you require and convert it to a markup.

Does the markup amount include seller fees?

No. The markup amount is gross profit before platform commissions, payment processing, shipping, and packaging. Subtract those before judging whether the price is profitable.

Why is a 75% markup not a 75% margin?

Because markup is measured against cost and margin against the selling price. A 75% markup on $12 adds $9 and produces a $21 price, and $9 is 42.9% of $21, not 75%.
