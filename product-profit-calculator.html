A product can look profitable on the price tag and still lose money. The gap usually comes from the costs that never appear next to the price: marketplace and payment fees, shipping and fulfillment, returned or damaged units, and the value of your own time. This calculator starts from the costs people remember — selling price and product cost — and adds the rest of the per-unit picture so the profit figure reflects what a sale actually contributes.

Why unit profit is the number to watch

Revenue is not profit. A product that sells for $24 and costs $8 to make looks like it clears $16 a unit, but that $16 is not yours yet. Fees take a percentage of the sale, shipping and fulfillment take a fixed amount, and some part of your overhead belongs to every unit you sell. Subtract all of them and the real contribution is smaller — and it is that smaller number, multiplied across a realistic sales volume, that decides whether a product is worth listing.

Unit profit also makes products comparable. Two items with very different prices, costs, and fee structures can be lined up on profit per unit to see which one actually rewards your effort.

Where a unit of profit goes

Between the price and the profit, several costs sit in line:

  • Product cost — materials and direct production, the most visible cost.
  • Percentage fees — marketplace commission or payment processing, taken as a share of the sale.
  • Shipping and fulfillment — the per-unit cost of getting the item to the buyer, whether you or a carrier does the work.
  • Allocated fixed cost — your share of the overhead that does not change per sale but still has to be covered by every sale.
  • Returns and spoilage — the cost of units that come back damaged or never sell, spread across the ones that do.

The first two are easy to see. The last three are where thin margins hide, and they are the reason a healthy price can still produce a weak profit.

How this calculator works

Profit per unit = selling price − product cost − percentage fees − shipping/fulfillment − allocated fixed cost  ·  total profit = profit per unit × expected units sold

The percentage fee is applied to the selling price, while product cost, shipping, and the allocated fixed cost are subtracted as flat per-unit amounts. Expected units sold scales only the total, not the per-unit figure. The margin is profit per unit divided by the selling price.

Worked examples

Using the default assumptions — $24 selling price, $8 product cost, 8% fees, $2 shipping, $1 allocated fixed cost, 100 units:

  • Default case → fees $1.92; profit $11.08/unit; margin 46.2%; 100 units give $1,108.00 total profit.
  • Price cut to $18, same costs → fees $1.44; profit $5.56/unit; margin 30.9%. Shipping and fixed cost do not shrink with the price.
  • Price lifted to $32, same costs → fees $2.56; profit $18.44/unit; margin 57.6%.
  • Cheaper sourcing to $5, price back at $24 → fees $1.92; profit $14.08/unit; margin 58.7%.

Notice how much of the outcome is set by the flat costs. Cutting the product cost by $3 adds exactly $3 of profit, while cutting the price by $6 removes more than $5 — because the fee falls with the price but shipping and overhead do not.

Common mistakes when calculating product profit

  • Counting only materials. A per-unit figure built from product cost alone will always look better than it is; fees, shipping, and overhead still have to come out of that same sale.
  • Not valuing your own time. If you spend an hour finishing each unit, that hour is a cost. Leaving it at zero makes an unprofitable product look fine.
  • Ignoring returns, breakages, and unsold stock. A small percentage of damaged or returned units adds a real per-unit cost to the ones that do sell.
  • Skipping the platform and payment fees. A percentage of the sale leaves your account before the profit does, and on cheap items the fixed portion of that fee can be the largest single cost.
  • Allocating no fixed cost. Setting overhead to zero flatters the per-unit profit, especially when the expected volume is low and the overhead has fewer units to spread across.

How to use the result

  • Compare products on profit per unit rather than on margin or price, so a cheap item with a high percentage can be judged on the same footing as a costly one with a high dollar figure.
  • Re-run the numbers at a conservative sales volume; a per-unit profit that only works at a hundred sales a month is a plan, not a result.
  • Use the figure as a price floor when a supplier raises costs or a marketplace changes its fees, and check whether the product still clears a margin you can live with.

Important assumptions

Results are planning estimates. The calculator applies the percentage fee to the selling price and subtracts the other costs as per-unit amounts; it does not include taxes, one-off launch costs, or your own labour unless you fold those into the product cost yourself. The allocated fixed cost is only as accurate as the figure you enter — decide deliberately how much overhead each unit should carry. Revisit the numbers whenever your costs, fees, or expected volume change.

What is product profit per unit?

It is the selling price minus every per-unit cost included in the calculation — product cost, percentage fees, shipping and fulfillment, and any allocated fixed cost. It shows how much one sale contributes after those costs.

Should I include my own labour in the product cost?

Yes, if you want the profit figure to be realistic. If you leave your time at zero, a product that depends on many hours of work can look profitable when it is not actually paying you for that time.

How do returns and damaged stock affect profit?

They raise the effective cost of the units that do sell. A simple way to model them is to spread the cost of returned or spoiled units across the units that sell, then enter that amount as part of the product cost.

Why does a lower price cut profit so sharply?

Because the fee falls with the price but shipping and allocated fixed cost do not. When those flat costs make up a larger share of a smaller sale, the profit that remains shrinks faster than the price does.

How can I improve product profit?

Change one lever at a time — price, unit cost, fulfillment, fee structure, or fixed-cost allocation — and compare the results. Altering several at once makes it hard to see which change actually helped.

Can I use this for my own business?

Yes. Enter your own numbers to build a planning estimate. The calculator is built for comparison and everyday decisions, not as an official accounting statement.
