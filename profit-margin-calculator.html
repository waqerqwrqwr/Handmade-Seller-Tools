REAL-WORLD EXAMPLE

Example: turning revenue and costs into a margin

If revenue is $5,000 and total costs are $3,250, profit is $1,750 and profit margin is 35%. Markup on cost is about 53.8%. These are different percentages because margin uses revenue as the denominator while markup uses cost.

Practical tip: Use your own costs and fee assumptions before making a pricing decision.

HOW IT WORKS

Measure profitability with margin and markup

Margin answers “how much of the selling price is profit?” Markup answers “how much did I add to cost?” Both are useful, but they are not interchangeable.

Margin uses revenue

Profit margin = profit ÷ revenue.

Markup uses cost

Markup = profit ÷ cost.

Target margin works backward

The target-margin price is cost divided by one minus the target margin.

FORMULA

Understand the calculation

Knowing the formula makes it easier to adjust the tool to your own business model.

Profit margin = (revenue − cost) ÷ revenue Markup = (revenue − cost) ÷ cost Price at target margin = cost ÷ (1 − target margin)

FAQ

Common pricing questions

What is a 50% profit margin?

A 50% margin means half of the final selling price is profit before any costs not included in the calculation.

Is 50% markup the same as 50% margin?

No. A 50% markup produces a lower margin. For example, $100 cost with a 50% markup gives a $150 selling price and a 33.3% margin.

Can margin be negative?

Yes. A negative margin means costs are greater than revenue.

Why does the target price rise quickly at high margins?

Because the denominator gets smaller as the target margin approaches 100%.
